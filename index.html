Overview

What Percolation Means

Percolation is a way to study how something moves through a system. In this project, the system is shown as a grid made of small squares. Each square can either be blocked or open. If enough open squares connect from the top of the grid to the bottom, then the system percolates. This means there is a complete path that allows something, like water or electricity, to travel through.

Example

Water and Electricity

A simple way to imagine percolation is water moving through soil. If there are enough connected empty spaces in the soil, water can pass through from the surface to deeper layers. If the spaces are blocked or disconnected, the water cannot flow all the way through. The same idea can also apply to electricity. If enough conductive materials are connected, electricity can travel through the object. If not, the material stays insulated.

Threshold

When a Path Appears

Percolation is important because it helps us understand when a connected path forms in a random system. At first, only a few spaces might be open, so nothing can move very far. But as more spaces open, the system eventually reaches a point where a full path suddenly appears. This point is called the percolation threshold.

Applications

Why It Matters

Percolation can model water draining through land, oil moving through rock, diseases spreading through populations, electricity passing through materials, or information spreading through networks. In computer science, it turns a flow problem into a connectivity problem.

What is Percolation?

What is Percolation?

Percolation is a simple way to study how things become connected. Imagine water being poured onto the top of a grid. At first, every square is blocked, so the water cannot move anywhere. When you click a square, that site becomes open. Water can move through open squares that touch each other from above, below, left, or right.

In this demo, blue squares are called full. A full square is an open square that is connected to the top row through a chain of other open squares. The system percolates when there is a connected open path from the top row all the way to the bottom row.

This model helps us understand connectivity in random systems. For example, it can represent water moving through soil, disease spreading through a population, electricity passing through a material, or information moving through a network.

How the Computer Tracks Connections

How the Computer Tracks Connections

Behind the scenes, the program keeps track of which open sites are connected. When a new site is opened, it is connected to any open neighbors above, below, left, or right. The system percolates when an open site in the bottom row becomes connected to an open site in the top row.

A common efficient way to implement this is with union-find, also called disjoint sets. Union-find keeps track of which open sites belong to the same connected component, so the program can quickly tell whether a path from top to bottom has formed.

Monte Carlo Simulation

Estimating the Percolation Threshold

Start with every site blocked. Then repeatedly choose one blocked site uniformly at random and open it. When the system first percolates, record the fraction of sites that are open. That fraction is one estimate of the percolation threshold.

By repeating this experiment many times and averaging the results, we get a more reliable estimate. The simulation below reports the sample mean, sample standard deviation, and a 95% confidence interval.

Central Limit Theorem Connection

Each trial of the percolation simulation gives a slightly different threshold because the sites are opened randomly. Some trials percolate earlier, and some percolate later.

The Central Limit Theorem states that when we repeat a random experiment many times, the average of the results becomes more stable and follows an approximately normal pattern. This means that even though one trial may be random, the average threshold from many trials gives a reliable estimate of the true percolation threshold.

Hence, we can use the sample mean and variation from the trials to create a 95% confidence interval. The more trials we run, the more accurate and trustworthy our estimate becomes.

Methodology

How the Project Works

This project models percolation on an N-by-N grid. Each site begins blocked. In the demo tab, users open sites directly and the system checks whether a connected path from top to bottom has formed. The implementation uses union-find connectivity to track both percolation and fullness efficiently.

Simulation

Monte Carlo Approach

To estimate the threshold, the program repeatedly opens random blocked sites until the system percolates. The fraction of open sites at that moment is recorded as one experimental estimate. Repeating this many times produces a distribution of threshold estimates.

Statistics

Why Repetition Matters

The sample mean provides an estimate of the percolation threshold, while the sample standard deviation measures how much the trials vary. The 95% confidence interval gives a reasonable range for the true threshold based on the simulated trials.

Discussion

What the Results Show

The threshold graph highlights a sharp transition: below a certain fraction of open sites, percolation is unlikely; above it, percolation becomes very likely. That steep change is the key phenomenon this project is designed to visualize.
